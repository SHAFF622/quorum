A referee for AI scientists

AI scientists work fast. Quorum checks their homework.

AI systems can now read thousands of papers and analyze patient data in a day. But nobody checks their conclusions automatically, so a human expert still has to re-read everything. Quorum is a panel of independent referees that re-checks every claim and hands each one a card.

Act on itRe-checked from the raw data and it holds up.
Check firstMight be right, but something needs a human look.
Set asideWrong, overstated, or impossible to verify.

The problem

The best AI scientist is right 4 times out of 5, and worst when it explains

Kosmos, built by Edison Scientific, is the most advanced AI scientist available. Its creators had experts grade 102 of its statements. It was good at doing the math and finding papers. It was much weaker at saying what the results mean.

Share of Kosmos statements that experts confirmed. Source: the Kosmos paper, arXiv 2511.02824.

In everyday terms: it's a brilliant intern who calculates correctly but jumps to conclusions. Its own authors write that "there exists no automated method to reliably evaluate if a claim is accurate, novel, and significant." Every report still needs a human expert to check about 25 claims per discovery.

How Quorum works

Five referees, each seeing only part of the picture

Each referee checks a claim in a different way and never sees how the others worked, so when they agree, it means something. A simple, fixed rule then gives the card. A referee can lower trust, but no AI can ever raise it on its own.

1 · The explorer

Asks questions of the data

An AI scientist studies the research question and writes its findings as plain sentences.

Like a student handing in an essay.

2 · The re-checker

Redoes the math blind

Reads only the sentence, works out how to test it, and recomputes it from the raw data.

Like a teacher solving the problem again without looking at the student's work.

3 · The rule-keeper

Catches classic mistakes

Too few patients to be sure, "causes" when the data only shows a link, or testing 40 things until one looks lucky.

Like a spell-checker, for statistics.

4 · The second opinion

Asks other patients

Re-runs the finding on a completely separate group of patients from a different study.

Like asking a second hospital if they see the same thing.

5 · The skeptic

Asks "does that follow?"

A senior-reviewer AI looks for a conclusion that goes further than the numbers allow.

Like the toughest person at the lab meeting.

Then · The card

A fixed rule decides

Plain code, not an AI, turns the evidence into green, yellow or red, and records why.

Like the scoring rules of a game, written down before anyone plays.

Case file · real data, 1,981 breast cancer patients

The trap: a finding that looks certain and points the wrong way

We pointed Quorum at a real open question in breast cancer: does the amount of estrogen receptor on a tumor matter, beyond the usual yes-or-no test? The estrogen receptor (measured by a gene called ESR1) is like an antenna on the cancer cell that picks up the hormone estrogen.

1

A naive analysis says: more antenna, more danger

Among 1,506 patients whose tumors respond to estrogen, more ESR1 goes with 19% higher risk of dying per step up, with odds of a fluke below 1 in 400,000. A system without referees would publish this.

2

The referees ask: who are these patients, and what did they die of?

Patients with the most ESR1 were also the oldest, and most of their deaths were from other causes. In this dataset, 497 of 1,144 deaths weren't from breast cancer at all.

Second opinion: same direction, not enough patients to be sure

In a separate study (TCGA, 676 patients), the result leans the same way (0.94×), but only 43 of them died of breast cancer. That's too few to confirm it, so Quorum gives it a Check first card: not confirmed, not disproved. Quorum says what it doesn't know.

What else the data says

Three open questions, checked honestly

Clear in the data
43% vs 30%

Ancestry and tumor genetics

Patients of African ancestry more often had a mutation in TP53, a gene that normally stops cells growing out of control, and twice as often the aggressive "basal" type (32% vs 15%).

Clear in the data
29% vs 9%

Young patients get tougher cancers

Women diagnosed under 40 had the aggressive basal type three times as often as older women.

Too few to say
16 deaths

Survival in small groups

Whether these groups also die sooner can't be settled here: the smallest groups have as few as 16 deaths. Quorum flags this instead of guessing.

Social and rural factors also matter for survival but aren't recorded in these public datasets, so Quorum can't study them.

Words, decoded

The jargon, in one line each

AI scientist
Software that picks research questions, analyzes data and reads papers on its own.
Claim
One sentence of findings, like "larger tumors go with worse survival".
Survival
How long patients live after diagnosis. "Overall" counts any death; "cancer-specific" counts only deaths from the cancer.
Risk multiplier
1.5× means one group dies 1.5 times as often over the same time; 1.0× means no difference.
Fluke odds (p-value)
How likely a result this strong would show up by pure chance.
Estrogen receptor (ESR1)
A sensor on some breast cancer cells that responds to the hormone estrogen. More sensor usually means hormone therapy works.
Confounding
A hidden third factor (here, age) that makes two things look linked when they aren't.
METABRIC and TCGA
Two large, free, public collections of breast cancer patient data used by researchers worldwide.

Limits

What Quorum can't do yet

  • It checks claims against data and papers. A lab experiment is still the final word.
  • The main dataset's patients were treated between 1977 and 2005, before some modern drugs. Quorum flags claims this affects.
  • The second dataset followed patients for only about two years, so it can confirm a direction more often than a size.
  • The test answers come from settled medicine and code, not from a fresh panel of doctors.
